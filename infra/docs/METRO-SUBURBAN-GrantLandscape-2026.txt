Applicable to: Cities 50,000–1,000,000+ population, Suburban municipalities in MSAs

SECTION 1: THE SUBURBAN ADVANTAGE IN FEDERAL GRANT COMPETITIONS
=============================================================================

Suburban and mid-size cities occupy a unique and powerful position in the
federal grant landscape. They are:
  - Large enough to have professional staff, capital reserves, and bond ratings
  - Small enough to generate compelling benefit-cost ratios (less existing infrastructure)
  - Less bureaucratically complex than major metros (faster project delivery)
  - Often overlooked by federal agencies who assume "suburban = wealthy = doesn't need help"

THE COMPETITIVE EDGE OF SUBURBAN CITIES:
1. FINANCIAL CREDIBILITY: Investment-grade bond ratings (Aa1, Aa2, A1) signal to
   federal reviewers that the city can execute and won't default on match commitments.
   A Moody's Aa2 or S&P AA rating is equivalent to a major Fortune 500 company's credit.

2. PROJECT READINESS: Suburban cities with CIPs often have projects at 30–60% design,
   environmental permits initiated, and right-of-way secured. Federal programs score
   readiness highly — a project that can break ground in 18 months vs. 3+ years scores better.

3. MATCH CAPACITY: Capital reserves of 15–20% of budget = credible, committed local match.
   Federal reviewers compare "available reserves" to "required match" — show the math.

4. TRACK RECORD: Prior federal awards with zero audit findings are a scoring differentiator.
   "Buffalo Grove has received and fully complied with $X in prior federal awards" closes deals.

5. PARTNER LEVERAGE: Suburban cities coordinate with counties, MPOs, transit agencies.
   Multi-governmental partnerships (3+ entities) consistently outscore solo applications.

6. DATA RICHNESS: Suburban cities in metro areas have abundant crash data, traffic counts,
   census data, environmental monitoring — the evidence basis federal reviewers require.

SECTION 2: HUD ENTITLEMENT PROGRAMS (Cities 50,000+)
=============================================================================

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
COMMUNITY DEVELOPMENT BLOCK GRANT (CDBG) — ENTITLEMENT
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Threshold: Cities ≥ 50,000 population OR principal cities of MSAs receive direct HUD allocation
Annual Allocation: Formula-based; typical suburban entitlement city: $300K–$3M/year
  Formula factors: Population (30%), poverty rate (30%), housing age pre-1940 (20%), other (20%)
  Example allocations: Chicago $58M; Naperville IL ~$700K; Aurora IL ~$2.5M; Buffalo Grove (below threshold, state CDBG)

WHAT ENTITLEMENT STATUS MEANS:
  - Annual guaranteed allocation from HUD (no competition)
  - Must submit 5-Year Consolidated Plan + Annual Action Plan to HUD
  - Must adhere to 70% LMI benefit rule, public participation requirements
  - Must maintain Analysis of Impediments to Fair Housing (AI) or Assessment of Fair Housing (AFH)

STRATEGIC USE OF CDBG IN SUBURBAN ENTITLEMENT CITIES:
  Public Facilities (25%): Community centers, senior centers, parks in LMI census tracts
  Housing (35%): Homeowner rehabilitation, code enforcement, emergency repairs for LMI households
  Economic Development (20%): Façade grants, commercial district improvements, business loans
  Public Services (15% cap): Senior services, homeless assistance, youth programming
  Planning & Admin (20% cap): Consolidated Plan updates, fair housing activities

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
HOME INVESTMENT PARTNERSHIPS (HOME)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Threshold: Participating Jurisdictions (PJs) = cities ≥ 65,000 + suburban consortia
Annual Allocation: Similar formula to CDBG; typical mid-size city: $300K–$2M/year
Required: 25% match from non-federal sources
Required CHDO Set-Aside: 15% of HOME allocation must go to Community Housing Development Organizations (CHDOs)
Best Uses:
  - Down-payment assistance programs for first-time LMI homebuyers
  - Homeowner rehabilitation for low-income households
  - Affordable rental housing development partnerships
  - Tenant-based rental assistance (TBRA)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
HOUSING TRUST FUND (HTF)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Recipients: States (not localities directly)
Purpose: Affordable housing for extremely low income (≤ 30% AMI) and very low income (≤ 50% AMI)
Benefit for cities: States pass through to developers building in your city
Strategy: Engage state housing finance agency to include your city in HTF allocations

SECTION 3: TRANSPORTATION PROGRAMS FOR SUBURBAN METRO CITIES
=============================================================================

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MPO PROGRAMMING — THE GATEWAY TO FEDERAL TRANSPORTATION FUNDS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CRITICAL INSIGHT: Most federal transportation dollars flow through Metropolitan
Planning Organizations (MPOs), NOT directly to cities. A city must work with its
MPO to get projects onto the Transportation Improvement Program (TIP/STIP) before
federal funds can be used.

HOW MPO PROGRAMMING WORKS:
  1. City develops project concept with technical justification (crash data, traffic counts, BCA)
  2. City submits project to MPO call for projects (STP, CMAQ, TAP — annual or biannual cycles)
  3. MPO technical committee scores and ranks projects
  4. Projects selected by MPO policy board for inclusion in TIP
  5. TIP projects are programmed into state STIP (Statewide TIP)
  6. Project becomes eligible for federal funds; city applies to state DOT for NOFA

KEY MPOs BY METRO AREA:
  Chicago: CMAP (Chicago Metropolitan Agency for Planning) — serves northeast Illinois
    Programs: STP-Urban, CMAQ, TAP; Call for Projects every 2–3 years
    Website: cmap.illinois.gov/programs/transportation-alternatives
  Dallas-Fort Worth: NCTCOG (North Central Texas Council of Governments)
  Houston: H-GAC (Houston-Galveston Area Council)
  Atlanta: ARC (Atlanta Regional Commission) — not to be confused with Appalachian RC
  Denver: DRCOG (Denver Regional Council of Governments)
  Seattle: PSRC (Puget Sound Regional Council)
  Phoenix: MAG (Maricopa Association of Governments)
  Boston: MAPC (Metropolitan Area Planning Council)
  Los Angeles: SCAG (Southern California Association of Governments)
  New York: NYMTC, NJTPA, CTDOT for tri-state
  Philadelphia: DVRPC (Delaware Valley Regional Planning Commission)
  Minneapolis: Met Council
  St. Louis: East-West Gateway Council of Governments
  Columbus: MORPC (Mid-Ohio Regional Planning Commission)

WHAT TO SUBMIT TO MPO:
  - Project purpose and need statement (problem statement with data)
  - Crash analysis (5-year crash data by type: fatal, injury, PDO)
  - Traffic volume counts (ADT, peak hour)
  - Pavement condition data (PCI scores) if resurfacing
  - Preliminary cost estimate by work type
  - Environmental screening (potential issues: historic, wetlands, floodplain)
  - Local match commitment (% of total project cost)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
RAISE GRANTS — STRONG FIT FOR SUBURBAN CITIES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
(See FEDERAL-MajorGrantPrograms for full RAISE details)
Suburban Sweet Spot: $3M–$15M projects that don't need MEGA scale but exceed STP threshold
Examples of winning suburban RAISE applications:
  - Intersection reconstruction (high-crash, dual left turns, adaptive signals)
  - Multimodal connector to transit station (pedestrian, bike, bus access)
  - Railroad grade separation (at-grade crossing elimination)
  - Downtown streetscape + pedestrian safety improvements
  - Freight access road to industrial park

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SAFE STREETS & ROADS FOR ALL (SS4A) — IDEAL FOR SUBURBS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Why SS4A is perfect for suburban cities:
  - Funding available for BOTH planning (Vision Zero action plan) AND implementation
  - Lower competition than RAISE for safety-specific projects
  - Excellent for suburbs that want to start with a safety study then implement
  - Priority given to communities with pedestrian/cyclist fatalities or near-misses
  - Strong equity scoring: can document vulnerable road users (school zones, senior centers)

Two-Step Strategy:
  1. Apply for SS4A Action Plan grant ($100K–$5M for planning/study) — lower competition
  2. Use the completed Action Plan as the roadmap for SS4A Implementation grant ($1M–$25M)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
STATE DOT PROGRAMS (Suburban Access)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Every state has multiple programs funneling federal funds to local roads.
For suburban cities, key access points:

Illinois (IDOT) Programs:
  - STP-Urban (via CMAP): Pavement, bridges, safety improvements in urban areas
  - HSIP: Safety improvements at high-crash locations; 90/10 federal/local match
  - ITEP: Transportation enhancement; trails, pedestrian, bike, scenic byways
  - BUILD Illinois Bonds: State infrastructure bonds for local roads
  - Rebuild Illinois (Rebuild): $4.1B capital program; competitive local road grants
  Illinois Local Roads Program: Contact IDOT District Office for local road funding

Texas (TxDOT) Programs:
  - Off-System Bridge Program: Bridge replacement/rehabilitation
  - Transportation Development Credit: Flexible local funding mechanism
  - Urban Transportation Program: For cities in urbanized areas
  - Surface Transportation Block Grant (STBG): Administered through TxDOT districts

California (Caltrans) Programs:
  - Active Transportation Program (ATP): Bike/pedestrian, state-administered
  - Local Partnership Program: Match state funds with local sales tax revenues
  - Solutions for Congested Corridors (SCCP): $250M/year for corridor improvements
  - HSIP Local: Highway safety for local agencies

Florida (FDOT) Programs:
  - Transportation Regional Incentive Program (TRIP): Regional significance projects
  - County Incentive Grant Program (CIGP): County road improvements
  - Small County Outreach Program (SCOP): For small counties < 100K population

SECTION 4: WATER & ENVIRONMENTAL PROGRAMS FOR METRO CITIES
=============================================================================

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EPA CLEAN WATER SRF — METRO CITY STRATEGIES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
For cities in metro areas, CWSRF is typically used for:
  - Combined Sewer Overflow (CSO) Long-Term Control Plan projects
  - Separate Sewer Overflow (SSO) elimination
  - Green Infrastructure (bioswales, permeable pavement, rain gardens — "green" CWSRF)
  - Stormwater master plan implementation
  - Watershed protection projects

DWSRF LEAD SERVICE LINE REPLACEMENT:
  IIJA: $15B over 5 years for lead service line replacement
  Priority: Communities with lead service lines (LSLs) — especially pre-1986 housing
  How to apply: Through state DWSRF program; submit to state project priority list
  Key note: Federal law now requires all PWS to inventory LSLs and pursue replacement
  Chicago region: IEPA administering; submit to IEPA annually (October deadline typical)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FEMA BRIC FOR SUBURBAN METRO CITIES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Suburban cities with flood risk are IDEAL BRIC applicants:
  Strengths: CRS participation (common in suburbs), active NFIP, current LHMP,
    strong match capacity, documented flood history
  Best project types:
    - Flood early warning systems (stream gauges, emergency alert upgrades)
    - Green infrastructure (constructed wetlands, bioretention, detention basins)
    - Utility hardening (backup generators for lift stations, pump hardening)
    - Culvert upsizing (undersized culverts causing repeated flood damage)
  Key scoring factor: CRS class participation adds priority points
  How to apply: Through state emergency management (IEMA in IL, TEM in TX, etc.)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS PROGRAMS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Section 205 (Small Flood Control Projects):
  Award: Up to $10M per project
  Federal share: 65% federal / 35% local
  Purpose: Flood damage reduction in areas too small for major civil works projects
  Application: Through Army Corps District office; annual authorization cycle
  Eligible: Stream channelization, detention basins, floodplain management

Section 408 Permits:
  NOT a grant — a modification permit to federal project (e.g., a Corps-built levee)
  Required if city wants to modify infrastructure near or within a federal project

Project Partnership Agreements (PPA):
  For major flood control: Full project development; can include NEPA and design
  Study Authority: Small General Investigations (SGI) for watershed studies

SECTION 5: ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT GRANTS FOR METRO SUBURBS
=============================================================================

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EDA GRANTS FOR SUBURBAN COMMUNITIES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EDA Public Works:
  Note: Traditional EDA programs favor distressed communities, but suburbs can qualify
    if they have a distressed sub-area OR a post-disaster economic impact
  EDA Supplemental Empowerment Zone grants: If community has EZ/Promise Zone designation
  EDA STEM Talent Challenge: For metro regions building STEM workforce pipeline
  IEDP (Investments for Economic Diversification): Manufacturing, tech, R&D investment support

EDA TECH HUBS (CHIPS Act Section 10622):
  Total: $10B over 5 years
  Purpose: Build regional tech ecosystems in communities outside traditional coastal hubs
  Key: Requires regional consortium (city + university + private sector + workforce)
  Best for: Mid-size metros not currently established tech hubs

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
OPPORTUNITY ZONE INVESTMENT STRATEGY
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Suburbs often have OZ-designated census tracts (commercial strips, transit-adjacent areas)
Strategy: Market the OZ designation to attract private investment into brownfields,
  mixed-use development, affordable housing
Key: Not a direct grant — cities can leverage OZ + CDBG + HOME + State TIF + EDA brownfields

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
BROWNFIELD REDEVELOPMENT (EPA + EDA)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EPA Brownfields Assessment Grant: Up to $500K to assess contaminated sites
EPA Brownfields Cleanup Grant: Up to $500K per site for cleanup
EPA Brownfields Multipurpose Grant: Up to $800K (combined assessment + cleanup)
EDA Brownfields Redevelopment: Can add economic development element after cleanup
State brownfield programs: Many states have supplemental funding (IL Clean Illinois, TX VCP)
Strategy: Package EPA Brownfields + EDA + OZ + LIHTC into a comprehensive redevelopment deal

SECTION 6: TECHNOLOGY & SMART CITY GRANTS
=============================================================================

NSF SMART & CONNECTED COMMUNITIES (S&CC):
  CFDA: 47.070
  Award Range: $1M–$2.5M
  Purpose: Develop and test technology-driven solutions to urban challenges
  Eligible: Municipal governments partnered with a university (NSF requires academic partner)
  Focus: Smart infrastructure (traffic sensors, IoT, predictive maintenance),
    data analytics, accessibility technology, emergency management AI
  Competition: Moderate — 30–50 applicants; strong if city has tech infrastructure
  Deadline: Annual NOFO; typically October–January

DOT AUTOMATION DISCRETIONARY GRANT (ADGP):
  Purpose: Pilot automated vehicle and advanced technology on public roads
  Award: $500K–$5M
  Best for: Progressive cities testing AV shuttles, smart intersections

DEPARTMENT OF ENERGY CITIES INITIATIVE:
  DOE Better Climate Challenge: Commit to 50% GHG reduction; receive technical assistance
  DOE Grid Modernization: Smart grid pilots for municipal utilities
  DOE Energy Efficiency Block Grant (EECBG): Energy audits, LED street lights, EV fleet

NTIA DIGITAL EQUITY:
  IIJA Funding: $2.75B
  Purpose: Address digital divide — devices, digital literacy, connectivity for LMI households
  Eligible: States, local governments, non-profits
  Strategy: Apply for Digital Equity Capacity Grants through state NTIA-designated entity

SECTION 7: HOUSING PROGRAMS FOR SUBURBAN CITIES
=============================================================================

AFFORDABLE HOUSING CHALLENGE FOR SUBURBS:
  Many suburban cities face workforce housing shortages (teacher, first-responder affordability)
  Key barrier: Zoning restrictions (single-family only) may conflict with some HUD programs
  Solution: HUD AFFH (Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing) requirements in CDBG/HOME
    cities require removing exclusionary zoning barriers

LIHTC (LOW INCOME HOUSING TAX CREDITS):
  Administered by State Housing Finance Agency (HFA) via Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP)
  Award: 9% or 4% tax credits for affordable rental housing development
  City Role: Approve sites, provide municipal support letters, offer land/fee waivers
  Key: City doesn't apply — developer applies; city positions itself as supportive jurisdiction

HOME DOWN-PAYMENT ASSISTANCE (DPA):
  HUD HOME funds used for down-payment assistance to LMI homebuyers
  Cities can run their own DPA programs or partner with county
  Income limit: ≤ 80% AMI for beneficiaries

SECTION 8 PROJECT-BASED VOUCHERS (PBV):
  PHAs can attach project-based vouchers to new affordable developments
  Suburban cities: Partner with PHA to bring PBVs into your jurisdiction (increases housing supply)

FEDERAL HOME LOAN BANK (FHLB) AFFORDABLE HOUSING PROGRAM:
  Member banks can sponsor affordable housing grants through FHLB
  Award: $750K–$5M per project through FHLB competitive program
  Access: Engage local community bank that is an FHLB member

SECTION 8: STATE-BY-STATE PROGRAM HIGHLIGHTS (METRO SUBURBS)
=============================================================================

ILLINOIS SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:
    - Illinois Commerce Commission Infrastructure Grants (water/sewer)
    - Rebuild Illinois: $4.1B bond program; local road, bridge, transit
    - DCEO CDBG: Non-entitlement cities apply to DCEO; priority: downtown revitalization
    - Illinois Housing Development Authority (IHDA): LIHTC, HOME, TIF-backed housing
    - Illinois Environmental Protection Agency (IEPA): CWSRF/DWSRF, NPS grants
    - IDOT STP/CMAQ via CMAP: Transportation improvements in northeast Illinois
  Regional: Chicago Metropolitan Agency for Planning (CMAP) — ONE Metropolitan Chicago Plan
  Unique: Illinois requires cities to complete a "grant certification" with GATA (Grant Accountability and Transparency Act) before applying to any state grant

TEXAS SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:
    - TxDOT Transportation Alternatives Program: Bike/ped, historic
    - TCEQ Water/Wastewater Financial Assistance (SWQAP)
    - Texas General Land Office (GLO) CDBG: Disaster recovery, infrastructure
    - Texas Water Development Board (TWDB): SRF financing + some grants
    - Comptroller Texas Enterprise Fund: Economic development (competitive/negotiated)
  Unique: Texas has extensive municipal utility district (MUD) structures — many infrastructure
    grants go through MUDs or regional water authorities rather than cities directly

CALIFORNIA SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:
    - Caltrans Active Transportation Program (ATP): $400M/year for bike/ped
    - California Water Boards CWSRF/DWSRF: Substantial funding with equity focus
    - HCD CDBG: Non-entitlement community development
    - California Strategic Growth Council: Climate adaptation, infill, transit-oriented development
    - CDFW Proposition 68: Parks and natural lands
    - AB 617 Community Air Protection: EJ communities with air pollution
  Unique: Prop 68 ($4B bond) and Prop 1 (water bond) provide state-specific funding outside federal

FLORIDA SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:
    - FDOT TRIP (Transportation Regional Incentive Program): Regional significance
    - CDBG via DEO: Housing, economic development for small/non-entitlement cities
    - FDEP SRF: Water/wastewater revolving fund
    - Florida Resilient Florida Grant Program: Flood protection, sea level rise adaptation
    - Visit Florida + DEOS Tourism Development: Tourism-adjacent infrastructure
  Unique: Florida Resiliency Grant (FL Senate created specifically for flood adaptation)

NEW YORK SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:

    - EFC (Environmental Facilities Corp) SRF: Water/wastewater
    - NY Homes & Community Renewal: CDBG, HOME, LIHTC
    - NY Works Capital Program: Broad infrastructure investment (allocated to localities)
    - NYSERDA: Energy efficiency, renewable energy for municipalities
  Unique: CHIPS (Consolidated Highway Improvement Program) — $438M/year to counties/cities

OHIO SUBURBAN CITY GRANT LANDSCAPE:
  Key State Programs:

    - ODSA CDBG: Non-entitlement cities
    - Ohio EPA SRF: Water/wastewater
    - Ohio DOT Urban Paving: Local road improvements
    - Third Frontier: Technology commercialization and economic development
  Unique: Ohio Public Works Commission (OPWC) — highly efficient infrastructure funding
    specifically for small/mid-size Ohio communities; very responsive to local applications

SECTION 9: GRANT COMPETITION ANALYSIS BY PROGRAM
=============================================================================

LOW COMPETITION (< 100 applicants nationally for suburban cities):
  - USDA Community Facilities (rolling, non-competitive)
  - SS4A Action Plan grants (< 100 suburban applicants)
  - HSIP through state DOT (state-level competition, typically 20–80 projects/state)
  - CWSRF/DWSRF (non-competitive; project priority list)
  - PROTECT formula (allocated to states; sub-allocated to localities)

MEDIUM COMPETITION (100–300 applicants nationally):
  - RAISE rural category (approximately 200 rural/suburban applicants)
  - FEMA BRIC (state-level competition, ~20–50 projects per state)
  - TAP/ITEP (state-level, ~30–100 projects per state)
  - STP-Urban via MPO (regional competition, ~20–60 projects per cycle)

HIGH COMPETITION (300+ applicants nationally):
  - RAISE urban category (400–600 applicants for 30–50 urban awards)
  - EDA Public Works (200–400 applications nationally)
  - HUD Choice Neighborhoods (50–80 applications for 2–4 awards)
  - NSF Smart Cities (50–100 applications for 8–12 awards)

STRATEGY: Target LOW and MEDIUM competition programs first to build track record,
then leverage track record in HIGH competition programs.
